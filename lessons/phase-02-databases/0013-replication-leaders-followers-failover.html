Phase 2.3 · Replication (part 1)

Replication: one leader, many followers, and what goes wrong when the leader dies

Keeping copies of a database on several machines is easy until something changes. This lesson covers how the common leader–follower setup copies changes, what "synchronous" really buys you, and why failover is the dangerous part.

Reading time ~20 min Main sources DDIA Ch. 5 "Leaders and Followers" · Alex Xu Ch. 1 "Database replication" Roadmap topics primary–replica · sync / async / semi-sync · read replicas · failover · split brain
What you'll be able to do Explain how a write travels from the leader to its followers, what synchronous, asynchronous and semi-synchronous replication each risk, and the four ways an automatic failover can go wrong. That turns "just add a replica" into an answer with its trade-offs stated.

01Why replicate, and what makes it hard

DDIA, Ch. 5 introduction — pp. 151–152

Replication means keeping a copy of the same data on several networked machines. Kleppmann gives three reasons: be close to users (lower latency), keep working when machines fail (availability, lesson 0003), and serve more reads (throughput). For now, assume every machine holds the whole dataset; splitting the data comes in 0016 · sharding.

If data never changed, you'd copy it once and be done. "All of the difficulty in replication lies in handling changes." Almost every distributed database uses one of three approaches: single-leader (this lesson and the next), multi-leader and leaderless 0015.

02Leader and followers

DDIA, "Leaders and Followers", Fig. 5-1 — pp. 152–153; Xu, "Database replication" — pp. 12–14
  1. One replica is the leader (also called master or primary). All writes go to it. It writes them locally first.
  2. The others are followers (read replicas, secondaries). The leader sends each change to them as a replication log, and they apply the changes in the same order.
  3. Reads can go to the leader or to any follower.

This is built into PostgreSQL, MySQL, Oracle and SQL Server, and into MongoDB. It's also how Kafka keeps its partitions safe Phase 4. Xu's interview version: "most applications require a much higher ratio of reads to writes", so you run one primary for writes and several replicas for reads, and get better performance, reliability and availability.

What read replicas don't fix

Every write still goes through one machine. Replicas scale reads, not writes. If the write rate is the problem, you need to split the data (0016 · sharding). Saying this unprompted in an interview shows you know where the limit is.

03Synchronous, asynchronous, semi-synchronous

DDIA, "Synchronous Versus Asynchronous Replication", Fig. 5-2 — pp. 153–155

The question is when the leader tells the client "done".

  • Synchronous follower: the leader waits until that follower confirms it has the write. That guarantees an up-to-date copy, but if that follower is slow or down, "the leader must block all writes". Making every follower synchronous would let any single outage halt the system.
  • Asynchronous follower: the leader sends the change and doesn't wait. Usually it arrives in under a second, but "there is no guarantee". Followers can fall minutes behind during recovery, overload or network trouble. If the leader dies, acknowledged writes that hadn't been copied are lost. "A write is not guaranteed to be durable, even if it has been confirmed to the client."
  • Semi-synchronous: exactly one follower is synchronous and the rest are async. If the sync one struggles, another is promoted to sync. Every acknowledged write is then on at least two machines.

Despite the risk, fully asynchronous is common, "especially if there are many followers or if they are geographically distributed", because the leader keeps accepting writes even when followers lag. Play with it:

Try: (1) fully asynchronous, write four times, let replication catch up once, write twice more, then crash the leader: some acknowledged writes are lost. (2) Switch to semi-synchronous and repeat: nothing acknowledged is lost. (3) Untick "fenced", crash, bring the old leader back and write: split brain.

04Adding a new follower without downtime

DDIA, "Setting Up New Followers" — pp. 155–156

You can't just copy the data files, because writes keep changing them during the copy. Instead: take a consistent snapshot without locking the database (the same snapshot mechanism as backups, lesson 0009), copy it to the new node, then replay every change since the snapshot. That only works because the snapshot is tied to an exact position in the leader's log: PostgreSQL's log sequence number, MySQL's binlog coordinates. Once the backlog is applied, the follower has "caught up".

05When nodes fail: catch-up is easy, failover is not

DDIA, "Handling Node Outages" — pp. 156–158

A follower fails: it knows the last change it applied, so after restarting it asks the leader for everything since then. That's simple.

The leader fails: a follower must be promoted, clients must send writes to it, and the other followers must follow it. This is failover. Done automatically, it has three steps: decide the leader is dead (in practice a timeout, "say, 30 seconds"), choose a new leader (usually the most up-to-date follower, by election or by a controller), and reconfigure everyone, including the old leader if it comes back. Kleppmann: failover "is fraught with things that can go wrong":

What goes wrongWhat happens
Lost writesWith async replication, the new leader lacks the old leader's last writes. "The most common solution… is simply discarded", even though clients were told they succeeded.
Other systems disagreeAt GitHub, an out-of-date MySQL follower became leader and reused auto-increment primary keys that the old leader had already handed out. Those keys were also used in Redis, so the two stores no longer matched, and "some private data [was] disclosed to the wrong users".
Split brainTwo nodes both believe they are leader and both accept writes, so data gets lost or corrupted. Some systems shut one node down (bluntly named STONITH, "Shoot The Other Node In The Head"), but a careless version can shut down both.
Wrong timeoutToo long means slow recovery. Too short means a load spike triggers an unnecessary failover, which "is likely to make the situation worse".

"There are no easy solutions", so "some operations teams prefer to perform failovers manually, even if the software supports automatic failover." You've seen the tools for this: timeouts and false deaths, the majority vote and fencing tokens from lesson 0006. Agreeing safely on a single new leader is consensus Phase 5 · Raft.

06What exactly is sent to followers?

DDIA, "Implementation of Replication Logs" — pp. 158–161
MethodWhat's shippedCatchUsed by

The logical log has a second life. Streaming a database's row changes to other systems (a warehouse, a search index, a cache) is change data capture (CDC) Phase 12.

In your world

Point heavy readers (training-data exports, analytics notebooks, feature backfills) at a read replica, not the primary that serves the product. Remember that the replica is asynchronous, so it may be seconds behind; the next lesson is about exactly that. And when a team says "we stream Postgres changes into the feature store", that's CDC built on the logical replication log.

07The page in five lines

  1. Single-leader: all writes go to the leader, which streams an ordered log to followers. Reads can go anywhere. Replicas scale reads, not writes.
  2. Async: fast and keeps working, but acknowledged writes can be lost on failover. Sync: safe, but one slow follower blocks writes. Semi-sync: one sync follower.
  3. New follower = consistent snapshot + replay from that snapshot's exact log position.
  4. Failover risks: lost writes, other systems going out of sync (GitHub's reused keys), split brain, wrong timeout. Many teams fail over manually.
  5. Replication logs: statement (nondeterminism), WAL (version-locked), logical row-based (decoupled, enables CDC), trigger (flexible, slow).
Optional: 30-second recall check 5 questions · click to open · not graded

A database is overloaded by writes. Will adding five read replicas help?

Single-leader replication scales reads only. Write throughput needs sharding.

With fully asynchronous replication, the leader dies right after acknowledging a write. What can happen?

The leader confirmed before any follower had the write. The new leader never saw it, and it's usually discarded.

What does semi-synchronous replication guarantee?

One follower is synchronous, so the leader plus that follower both hold every acknowledged write.

Why can't PostgreSQL's WAL shipping usually run different database versions on leader and followers?

A physical log is coupled to the storage engine's layout, so zero-downtime version upgrades are hard.

After a failover, the old leader returns and still accepts writes. What is this called?

Two leaders accepting writes is split brain. Fencing or a newer term/epoch number must make the old leader step down.
