Putting Phase 2 together: how a distributed key-value store is designed

Alex Xu's "design a key-value store" chapter uses almost every idea from the last eleven lessons. This lecture walks through it piece by piece, links each piece to the lesson that explained it, and marks where the book simplifies.

Reading time ~20 min Main source Alex Xu Ch. 6 "Design a Key-Value Store" (based on Dynamo and Cassandra), cross-checked with DDIA Chs. 3, 5, 6 Covers lessons 0007–0017
What you'll be able to do Explain a Dynamo/Cassandra-style key-value store end to end, from the hash ring to the bytes on disk, and say why each part is there and what it gives up. You'll also have a checklist for reviewing any data-layer design, including one proposed by a coding agent.

01The brief

Xu, Ch. 6, "Understand the problem and establish design scope" — pp. 87–89

Support put(key, value) and get(key), with small values (< 10 KB), a large dataset, high availability ("responds quickly, even during failures"), automatic scaling, tunable consistency and low latency. One machine with an in-memory hash table (plus compression and spilling cold data to disk) fills up "very quickly", so the data has to be spread over many machines. Xu also calls this a distributed hash table. Notice how much that brief already decides: availability over strict consistency, a key-only access pattern (so no joins or secondary indexes), and growth by adding machines.

02The first fork: consistency or availability

Xu, "CAP theorem", Figs. 6-1 to 6-3 — pp. 90–92

Xu frames it with CAP. When a replica is cut off, either block writes until the replicas agree (CP, as a bank would) or keep accepting reads and writes and reconcile later (AP). For this brief he picks AP and eventual consistency, like Dynamo and Cassandra. That's the right call. Read it with lesson 0004 in mind, though: "pick two of three" and "CA systems" are a loose way to put it. The real choice is what to do during a partition, and PACELC adds the everyday trade-off between latency and consistency, which is exactly what tunable w and r control.

04One request, end to end

Xu, "System architecture diagram", "Write path", "Read path", Figs. 6-17 to 6-21 — pp. 104–107

The architecture is fully decentralized: any node can act as the coordinator for a request, every node has the same responsibilities, and there's no single point of failure. A put is hashed onto the ring, sent to the key's n replicas, written to each replica's commit log and memtable, and acknowledged once w replicas confirm. A get asks the replicas, waits for r answers, returns the newest version and repairs any stale copy. Trace it:

Six servers, n = 3. Things to see: (1) put, take s2 down, put again: s5 holds s2's copy with a hint. Bring s2 back: hinted handoff. (2) Reset, untick sloppy quorum, set r = 1, then put, take s2 down, put, bring s2 back, get: a stale read, because w + r = n. (3) Put and get on other keys: each key has its own three replicas.

05What this design is not good at

Knowing the limits matters as much as knowing the parts. This design deliberately gives up:

  • Queries other than by key. No joins and no ad-hoc filters. Secondary indexes on a sharded store mean scatter/gather or lagging global indexes (0016).
  • Transactions across keys. There's no isolation level here at all (0009, 0010). "Check the balance, then spend" across two keys is write skew waiting to happen.
  • Read-your-writes and monotonic reads aren't guaranteed (0014). The application has to add them if it needs them.
  • Counters and read-modify-write under concurrent writers: last-write-wins drops updates, and vector clocks push the merging onto the client.

That's why the same company usually also runs a relational database (0011), and why "Cassandra for everything" is as wrong as "Postgres for everything".

In your world

This is the architecture behind many online feature stores and prediction logs: lookups by key at low latency, huge write volume, availability over perfect freshness. A model reading features at serving time gets "usually fresh" data. If a feature must reflect a write the same request just made, that's read-your-writes, and you design for it explicitly (read the source of truth, or pass the value along with the request). The same checklist applies when an agent proposes a vector store, a cache or a metadata database for your ML platform.

07Phase 2 in five lines

  1. On one machine: LSM-trees for writes, B-trees for reads; indexes shaped by the queries; transactions with an isolation level you've chosen on purpose, not the default by accident.
  2. Data shape: choose the model by its relationships; evolve schemas so old and new code can both read the data.
  3. Copies: single-leader is simplest; async replication lags and can lose acknowledged writes; leaderless trades order for availability and needs conflict handling.
  4. Splits: shard by the key most queries use, watch for hot keys, never use % N, and accept that cross-shard work costs more.
  5. A Dynamo-style key-value store combines all of these, and every part comes with a stated trade-off. That's the level of answer interviewers want.
Optional: 30-second recall check 5 questions · click to open · not graded

In the key-value store, what handles a replica that's down for a few minutes?

Temporary failures: a stand-in node keeps the write with a hint and hands it back later. Merkle trees are for permanent divergence.

Why is there no failover step in this design?

Leaderless replication: writes go to all n replicas and need only w acknowledgements, so a dead node doesn't need replacing as leader.

Which requirement would push you away from this design toward a relational database?

This store has no cross-key transactions or joins. The other three are exactly what it's built for.

What happens on each replica when it receives a write?

The LSM write path from lesson 0007: log first for crash safety, memtable for speed, flushed later as an SSTable.

An agent's design says "Cassandra with QUORUM reads and writes, so balances are strongly consistent." What's the flaw?

Quorums make reads usually fresh, but LWW, sloppy quorums and partial writes break the guarantee, and "check balance, then spend" needs real isolation.
