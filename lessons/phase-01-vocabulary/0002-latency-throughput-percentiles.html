Phase 1.2 · Performance

Latency, throughput, and why the average lies

How to describe "fast", and why the slowest 1% of requests matters more than the typical one.

Reading time ~18 min Main sources DDIA Ch. 1 "Describing Performance" · Alex Xu Ch. 2 Roadmap topics latency, throughput, QPS, bandwidth, concurrency, response time, tail latency, p50–p99.9
What you'll be able to do Read a latency dashboard correctly. Explain why p99 matters more than the average, why one slow server can slow down most of your users, and why a server at 90% CPU feels slow even though it's "only" 90% busy.

01Two different questions: "how much?" and "how fast?"

DDIA, Ch. 1, "Describing Performance" — p. 13

Kleppmann splits performance into two kinds of number:

  • Throughput: how much work gets done per second. For a web service it's usually counted as QPS or RPS. For a batch job, it's records per second or "how long does the whole job take".
  • Response time: how long one request takes, from the client sending it to the client getting the answer.

Which one you care about depends on the system. In his words, for batch systems "we usually care about throughput", while "in online systems, what's usually more important is the service's response time."

A third word gets mixed up with throughput: bandwidth. Bandwidth is the maximum amount of data a link can carry per second (e.g. a 1 Gbps network card). Throughput is how much actually gets through. Think of a highway: bandwidth is the number of lanes, throughput is how many cars actually pass per hour, and response time is how long one car's trip takes. A wide highway can still have a slow trip if there's a traffic jam.

In your world

Nightly batch scoring is a throughput problem: nobody cares how long row #4,812,330 took, only that the job finishes by 6 a.m. A real-time fraud check during checkout is a response-time problem: each request has to come back in, say, 50 ms. Many interview mistakes start by optimising the wrong one of these two.

Latency vs response time

DDIA sidebar "Latency and response time" — p. 14

Kleppmann is careful here. Response time is what the client sees: the actual work (the service time) plus time spent waiting in queues plus network travel. Latency, strictly, is only the time a request spends waiting to be handled. In everyday engineering talk (and in interviews), people say "latency" when they mean response time, and this course does the same. Just keep in mind that response time = waiting + working + travelling, because the "waiting" part is where most surprises come from (section 05).

02Response time is a distribution, not a number

Send the same request 100 times and you'll get 100 slightly different times. Kleppmann lists the usual causes, none of them under your control: a context switch to a background process, a lost network packet that has to be re-sent by TCP, a garbage-collection pause, a page fault, even "mechanical vibrations in the server rack." So most requests are fast and a few are randomly very slow.

Mean, median, percentiles

DDIA — p. 15
  • Mean (average): add them all up and divide. Kleppmann: it's "not a very good metric if you want to know your 'typical' response time, because it doesn't tell you how many users actually experienced that delay." A few very slow requests drag it up, so it describes nobody.
  • Median = p50: half the requests were faster than this, half slower. This is the "typical" experience.
  • p95, p99, p99.9 (written p999): 95%, 99% or 99.9% of requests were faster than this. "p99 = 1.2 s" means 1 in 100 requests took 1.2 s or longer.
  • Tail latency: the name for these high percentiles: the slow "tail" end of the distribution.

03Why the slow 1% matters so much

"It's only 1 in 1,000 requests" sounds harmless. Kleppmann gives three reasons it isn't.

1. Each user sends many requests. The median describes a single request. One page load, or one session, makes many requests, so "the probability that at least one of them is slower than the median is much greater than 50%." If loading a page takes 20 requests, most users hit the p95 at least once on every page.

2. The slowest requests often come from your best customers. Amazon sets its internal response-time targets at p99.9, because the slowest requests tend to be from customers "who have the most data on their accounts because they have made many purchases — that is, they're the most valuable customers." Amazon also observed that adding 100 ms of response time reduced sales by 1%.

3. Contracts are written in percentiles. A service's target might be "p50 under 200 ms and p99 under 1 s". These targets are called SLOs and SLAs. Nobody writes them in terms of the mean.

But don't chase every nine

Amazon decided that improving the p99.99 (1 in 10,000) was "too expensive" and not worth it. Very high percentiles are dominated by random events you can't control, and each extra nine costs more for less benefit. A strong interview answer names which percentile the requirement is about and why, e.g. "p99 under 200 ms for the checkout fraud check".

04Fan-out: how one slow server slows most users

DDIA, "Percentiles in Practice" — p. 16. The original paper: Dean & Barroso, "The Tail at Scale" (CACM 2013).

Real systems rarely answer a request alone. Your API calls a user service, a feature store and a model server. A search query asks 50 index shards at once. This is fan-out: one user request turns into many backend calls. Even when those calls run in parallel, the user has to wait for the slowest one.

If each call is slow 1% of the time, the chance that at least one of N calls is slow is 1 − 0.99N. Kleppmann calls this tail latency amplification. Google's Dean and Barroso give the famous example: with 100 servers that are each slow 1 time in 100, 63% of user requests are slow.

The slider shows how quickly "rarely slow" becomes "usually slow." Hover over the line to read any value.
The consequence

With wide enough fan-out (around 70 calls, since 1 − 0.9970 ≈ 50%), each backend's p99 becomes the user's p50. That's why big systems care so much about backend tail latency, and why techniques for cutting the tail exist. One example from the same paper is hedged requests. When you see a design with wide fan-out, the first question to ask is: "what's the tail latency of each leg?"

In your world

A RAG request that queries 8 vector-index shards, a feature lookup across 4 stores before a model call, an ensemble of 5 models: each is fan-out, and each user-facing p99 is set by the worst leg. RAG pipelines are especially prone to this because they chain fan-out stages one after another.

05Latency vs throughput: the queue between them

Latency and throughput aren't independent. Two ideas connect them.

Idea 1: Little's Law — how many requests are in flight

Not in DDIA or Xu. It's a classic queueing result. See Little's law.

On average, in a stable system:

requests in flight  =  throughput  ×  response time
     (concurrency)        (req/s)        (seconds)

If your API handles 2,000 requests/sec and each takes 50 ms, then on average 2,000 × 0.05 = 100 requests are in progress at any moment. So you need at least 100 worker threads, connections or async slots. This is how concurrency relates to the other two numbers. It's also a quick sanity check in capacity estimates, and a warning: if response time doubles (say the database slows down), the number of requests in flight doubles too, and you can run out of threads or connections even though traffic didn't change.

Idea 2: as a server gets busier, waiting time explodes

The curve uses the simplest queueing model (M/M/1: random arrivals, one worker): response time = work ÷ (1 − utilization). Real systems differ in detail but have the same shape.

Kleppmann notes that "queueing delays often account for a large part of the response time at high percentiles." A server can only work on a few things at once (limited by its CPU cores), so extra requests wait in line. A few slow requests at the front hold up the fast ones behind them, which is called head-of-line blocking. How long the line gets depends on how busy the server is, and the relationship is not linear:

Each request needs 50 ms of real work. Drag from 50% to 80% to 95% busy. The work never changes. Only the waiting does.

Going from 50% to 80% busy multiplies response time by 2.5. Going from 80% to 95% multiplies it by 4 more. This is why you can't run servers near 100% utilization and expect good latency, and why capacity plans deliberately leave headroom at peak instead of aiming for full machines. Throughput and latency trade against each other: squeezing the last bit of throughput out of a machine costs you response time.

In your world

GPU inference shows the same trade-off on purpose: batching requests multiplies throughput, but each request waits for its batch to fill. Serving systems expose exactly this as a setting: "max batch size" and "max wait time" are a throughput-vs-latency knob.

06Measuring it without fooling yourself

DDIA — p. 16

Three practical rules from Kleppmann that commonly trip up real dashboards:

  1. Measure on the client side. The server's own timer starts when it picks up the request, so it misses the time the request spent waiting in the queue. That queue time is exactly the part that grows under load.
  2. Never average percentiles. Averaging the p99 of 10 servers, or averaging minute-by-minute p99s into an hourly p99, is "mathematically meaningless." The right way is to combine the underlying histograms and compute the percentile from the total. Monitoring tools use compact structures for this, such as t-digest or HdrHistogram.
  3. Load-test honestly. If your load-testing tool waits for each response before sending the next request, it slows down exactly when the system does. The queue never builds up the way it would with real users, and your test looks better than reality. Real load keeps arriving whether or not you're slow.

07Numbers to know: how slow is "slow"?

Alex Xu, Ch. 2, Table 2-2 — open in your PDF. Updated, interactive version (Xu's own reference): Colin Scott's latency chart.

To reason about where time goes, you need a rough sense of how long things take. Xu reproduces Jeff Dean's (Google) well-known table from 2010. The exact numbers have changed since then, but the ratios between them are what matter. The last column rescales everything so an L1 cache read takes one second, to make the gaps easier to feel.

Xu's takeaways: memory is fast and disk is slow; avoid disk seeks; simple compression is fast, so compress before sending over the network; data centers in different regions are far apart in time. Two things to add:

  • Modern SSDs have largely replaced spinning disks for databases. A random read takes tens of microseconds, not 10 ms (see the interactive chart). The order of things still holds: memory ≪ SSD ≪ spinning disk.
  • The speed of light doesn't improve. A cross-ocean round trip is still ~150 ms. If users are on another continent, no amount of server tuning beats moving data closer to them, which is the whole idea behind CDNs and multi-region designs.

08The page in five lines

  1. Throughput = work per second. Response time = how long one request takes (waiting + working + travelling). Bandwidth = the link's maximum capacity.
  2. Response time is a distribution. Report p50 for typical and p95/p99/p999 for the tail. The mean describes nobody.
  3. Tail latency matters because users send many requests, slow requests often belong to your heaviest users, and SLOs are written in percentiles.
  4. Fan-out amplifies the tail: 1 − (1 − p)N. With wide fan-out, each backend's p99 becomes the user's p50.
  5. In-flight requests = throughput × response time (Little's Law). As utilization approaches 100%, queueing makes response times explode, so keep headroom.
Optional: 30-second recall check 5 questions · click to open · not graded

A dashboard shows mean = 180 ms, p50 = 90 ms. What does that most likely tell you?

When the mean is far above the median, a small number of slow outliers is dragging it up. The typical request is ~90 ms.

One user request calls 50 backends in parallel. Each is slow 1% of the time. Roughly what share of user requests are slow?

0.99⁵⁰ ≈ 0.605, so ≈ 39.5% of user requests hit at least one slow backend. Running the calls in parallel doesn't help, because you wait for the slowest.

You have 10 servers and want the fleet-wide p99 for the last hour. Which is correct?

Percentiles can't be averaged. Combine the underlying distributions (histograms) and compute the percentile from the combined data.

Your API does 1,000 req/s and each request takes 200 ms. On average, how many requests are in flight?

Little's Law: in-flight = throughput × response time = 1,000/s × 0.2 s = 200.

A team runs servers at 95% CPU to save money, and p99 latency is terrible even though each request's work is cheap. Why?

Near full utilization, queueing delay grows sharply. The work per request is the same; the waiting is what explodes.
